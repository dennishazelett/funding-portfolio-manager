# Portfolio Decision Rules

## Keep an item visible when

- an application is being developed;
- drafting is complete but submission has not occurred;
- it has been submitted and awaits review;
- it is scored and awaits a funding decision;
- a resubmission is concretely planned;
- a collaborator decision is pending and controls whether the opportunity proceeds;
- a watch item has a specific future reassessment point or concrete disease/cohort relevance.

## Retire or remove an item when

- the user has decided not to pursue it;
- its deadline passed without submission and no resubmission/reopening path matters;
- it was only a generic search result with no concrete fit;
- it is a completed development task rather than an active application;
- it is a hypothetical trainee/personnel mechanism with no actual trainee/application;
- a stronger mechanism has superseded it and no collaborator or strategic decision remains.

Retirement does not mean deleting submission history from the repository. It means removing non-current material from the current-state portfolio.

## Opportunity priority

### Priority
Natural scientific fit, meaningful award, feasible timeline, concrete collaborators/data, and strong strategic value.

### Secondary
Scientifically useful and feasible, but subordinate to higher-value submissions or only worth pursuing if development burden remains low.

### Watch
Potential future fit, but no current application decision. Keep only with a concrete reassessment trigger.

### Hold
A specific unresolved decision controls pursuit, often collaborator feedback, eligibility, data access, or program-contact guidance.

### Retire
Expected value no longer justifies effort or the opportunity is obsolete.

## Application burden

Treat application burden as a real portfolio cost. Consider:

- pages/materials required relative to award size;
- mandatory review service or other applicant obligations;
- membership/fees;
- compressed deadlines;
- administrative complexity;
- overlap with higher-priority deadlines;
- need to create substantial new preliminary work;
- collaborator enthusiasm and sponsor standing within the relevant field.

Do not present unverified perceptions of favoritism or sponsor culture as facts. Translate them into appropriately qualified expected-value considerations.

## Distinctness versus relabeling

A new disease label is insufficient. Seek material differences in several dimensions: question, biology, data, validation, endpoints, collaborators, interpretation, or translational consequence.

A different NOFO can legitimately support a related program when it changes the scientific architecture—for example, a methods-centered computational project versus a disease-centered program in which computational prioritization feeds experimental functional validation.

## Submission-capacity planning

Verify current sponsor limits before relying on a numeric cap. Once verified:

- track applications that count toward the cap;
- distinguish PI/multi-PI from roles that do not count under the governing rule;
- use available capacity to diversify strong A0s when strategically justified;
- preserve later flexibility to select A1s based on actual review outcomes;
- never create weak proposals solely to exhaust the cap.
